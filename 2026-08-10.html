Systems & tools

A payment that had already left the account was being subtracted twice, so every projected day-end read $4,627.91 low
The cash projection starts from the balance typed off the bank screen each morning, and each payment it expects is either matched against a real bank line or carried forward to the next day. Two payments — a $4,288.68 mortgage and a $339.23 loan — were dropped from the expected list because they had posted, and the same amounts were also taken off the typed balance, so the money came out twice. The direction was cautious, which is exactly why it was easy to miss: the figures looked worse than reality rather than better, and no day dropped anywhere near the floor either way. The starting figure has been reset to the bank's own balance, the whole five-day chain rebuilt from scratch, and then checked again after the system restarted — a correction that does not survive a restart has not really been made. This report will not build at all if the projection's starting figure and the bank balance disagree by so much as a cent.
The bank's own wording carries the card number, so spend can be split by card without anyone tagging it by hand
Every debit-card line on the checking statement ends in four digits, and those four digits say which card was used. Tested against QuickBooks' own account assignment on six transactions, it matched on all six — the five charges ending 0126 are all filed to one card in the books, and the one ending 2688 to the other. Until now, separating spend by card meant somebody remembering to label each line, which is slow and quietly goes wrong whenever a line is missed. It can now be read straight off the bank data. One caution is built in: a four-digit group that appears only once can be a store number rather than a card, so only a number that recurs is treated as one.
