Systems & tools

Payroll now sits in the cash projection as one line per check, not one weekly lump
Thirteen checks were written for the week and they do not all clear on the same day, so carrying the whole week as a single amount on a single date made the days either side of payday read wrong in both directions. Each check is now its own line, placed on the day that check has actually been clearing across the last three runs — the office checks on the day of payment, the crew in bulk on the Friday, and two consistent stragglers the Monday after. The thirteen lines add up to the recorded net of $15,703.24 to the cent, so the projection and the payroll record cannot drift apart.
A safety limit now sits in front of the overhead rate before it can reach any profit figure
The rate is stored as a fraction — 0.36 for 36%. Entered as 36 it would have been stored as a rate a hundred times too large and would have silently re-scored every RBA job, moving the profit figures FD and Rafael read. Nothing stopped that on the way in. A range check now does, and it had to be built per figure rather than as one blanket rule: the labour burden multiplier is legitimately above 1, so a single range would have rejected a valid figure and accepted a damaging one. 114 automated checks pass, including ones written for exactly this mistake.
The overhead rate is now a measurement on a schedule instead of a judgement call
It comes from the overhead calculator, on the same basis every time, re-measured every three months — next due early November. Its RBA revenue input was checked against the bank and ties to the cent: $512,997.22 of Andersen deposits through July 24, the date the calculator declares for itself. The difference of $18,281.48 against the payment sheet turned out to be a date, not a disagreement — one deposit of $18,940.00 landed on July 31, after the calculator's cut-off. Refreshing revenue without refreshing the overhead pool would push the rate down for no real reason, so both sides move together at the quarterly re-measure or not at all.
Job profit stops where the hours stop, and the reason is a handover between two uploads
Every one of the 3,215 time entries carries its job code, and 9,574.72 hours are costed to jobs for 2026 — but the job side of that data was last loaded on July 17. One export closes today's gap; keeping the job code on the weekly upload closes it for good.
