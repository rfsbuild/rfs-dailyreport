The payroll system was matching one worker's name to a record that had been switched off
Payroll starts from a time-tracking export, and each name on it has to be matched to a person on the payroll. One of those matching rules pointed the name "Alice" at a company record that had been switched off months ago and is paid a flat salary rather than by the hour — and because the rule was checked first, the correct match was never even attempted. A second rule beside it named a person who is not on the payroll at all, spelled differently from anyone real. Both were corrected before this week's run, and the result was checked on the actual checks rather than in a test: the hours went to the right person, at the right hourly rate, on check #3865. The switched-off record was also locked so that resetting the system can never bring it back as active, which is the route by which someone who has left can end up being paid.
Nine payments counted twice in our records, worth $2,469.72
When a payment is still pending, the bank often shows it under a generic description with no merchant name. When it settles a day or two later it appears again, properly named — and until now our records kept both, so the same money came out twice on paper. Comparing our records against the bank's own file line by line over the last two and a half weeks found nine of these, together worth $2,469.72 that our records are short against the bank. Each one was confirmed by the same test rather than by judgement: the generic line is absent from the bank's current file while the named one is present, which is only possible if they were the two halves of one charge. The direction is the safe one — the records read low, not high — and nothing has been removed yet, because deleting bank history is a decision worth making deliberately.
